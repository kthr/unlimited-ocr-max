<|det|>header [105, 114, 305, 129]<|/det|>R. Mallory and P. Sandoval
<|det|>text [104, 173, 444, 273]<|/det|>The instrument was designed for a river and was used in an estuary, and most of what follows is a consequence of that one decision. A float gauge measures the height of a surface that it assumes is flat and slow. Neither assumption holds within an hour of high water.
<|det|>text [104, 293, 490, 394]<|/det|>The counterweight is the part that fails first. It hangs in a stilling well of fixed bore, and the well damps the surface but does not damp the wind, so on the exposed stations the tape develops a slow oscillation with a period of about forty seconds and an amplitude that the chart cannot resolve.
<|det|>text [104, 413, 476, 513]<|/det|>What the chart records instead is a thickened trace. Reading the centre of a thickened trace is a judgement, and two observers reading the same week of chart differ by up to four centimetres in the thickest passages. That figure is from the 1966 intercomparison and has never been bettered.
<|det|>text [104, 533, 482, 650]<|/det|>The pressure transducers fitted at stations three and five from 1968 do not have this problem and have a different one. They drift. The drift is close to linear over a season and is removed by the datum checks, but the checks are three months apart and the removal is therefore an interpolation over a quantity nobody measured.
<|det|>text [104, 669, 493, 770]<|/det|>Neither instrument is preferred here. The float record is noisy and unbiased; the transducer record is smooth and carries an unknown low-frequency term. Where both exist they agree in the mean to within a centimetre and disagree in any single reading by rather more.
<|det|>text [104, 789, 478, 856]<|/det|>The bubbler at station six is the only instrument in the network that was calibrated in place rather than on the bench, and it is the only one whose certificate names the person who did it.
<|det|>text [104, 875, 490, 907]<|/det|>A word about the charts themselves. They were kept in the huts until 1971 and in the office afterwards,
<|det|>text [523, 173, 892, 223]<|/det|>and the huts were not dry. Three of the 1963 rolls have a tide line across them at about the same height, which is not a record of anything in the river.
<|det|>text [523, 242, 901, 343]<|/det|>Below that line the ink has spread and the trace is a band rather than a line. It has been read anyway, at the centre of the band, and the readings are flagged in the reduction. There are eleven weeks of them and they are the only readings in the series that carry a flag.
<|det|>text [523, 361, 915, 429]<|/det|>The office copies made in 1972 are photographic and reproduce the tide line faithfully, so nothing was gained by the copying except that the originals stopped being handled. Both sets survive.
<|det|>text [523, 447, 915, 530]<|/det|>Datum is the older of the two available and is not the national one. It sits four hundred and six millimetres below the national datum at reference mark one, and that offset has been checked twice, in 1964 and in 1970, and did not move between the checks.
<|det|>text [523, 550, 892, 650]<|/det|>Every height in this report is on the survey datum. Converting is a single subtraction and we have deliberately not done it, because a converted series that is later found to have used the wrong offset cannot be recovered, and an unconverted one always can.
<|det|>text [523, 669, 908, 753]<|/det|>The reference marks themselves are cut benchmarks on masonry and are still there. Reference mark two was reported destroyed in 1968 and was found again in 1974 under a bank of shingle, undamaged and at the same height to within two millimetres.
<|det|>text [523, 772, 905, 839]<|/det|>What follows is organised by instrument rather than by station, which makes the record of any one station harder to read and the record of any one failure mode much easier. A station index is given at the end.
